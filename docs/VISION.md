# The inventory, and everything around it

*A user story in the old sense — not acceptance criteria, but the way this thing should feel. Written 2026-11-20, the night Chrome 2026 landed on the platform.*

---

It's Thursday evening and a Chrome 2026 case break starts in an hour.

Your laptop shows the app. Not a spreadsheet, not seven tabs — one page that already knows what it's looking at. The harness spent the afternoon doing what it does: it read every Whatnot board on tonight's schedule out of `breakdata`, priced each one against the same database that served your morning questions — 25,091 Chrome 2026 variants, the full refractor ladder with real print runs, the odds sheet's 588 labels across eight box formats — and pushed the boards into the app with their edges computed. Every spot on every board has three numbers now: what the room is asking, what the cards inside are actually worth, and the gap between them.

You scroll. Most boards are noise, and the app says so — the whole-board ratio column makes the overpriced rooms visible at a glance, a `.20` discount on effective asks where the house habitually rounds in your favor. But two boards carry the flag. A team break on the Raiders board: Mendoza's Lavender Refractor is `/299` and the sell sheet says the parallel falls one in every 115 hobby-box equivalents; the spot asks €31, the card value behind it is €40, and the app has drawn the line through it. You take the spot.

Tomorrow the cards arrive. You tap them into the app on your phone between pickups — player, card #, the parallel off the back, and the app completes the sentence. It knows what `/299` means without being told; it knows Mendoza's Chrome checklist the way it knows Midnight's 7,543 variants. The value column fills itself from comps: 43,770 Flagship cards, 24,220 Chrome 2025s, the 1990 Score set your father's generation traded — 4,600 priced rows older than some of the sellers at the show. When a number appears, it says where it came from and when it was observed. A value without a source is a bug.

Sunday a package sells on eBay. You record the sale in fifteen seconds — price, fees, shipping — and the profit line updates, per card, per year, per channel. The realized number walks into the same database that priced the card, so next month's comps know what things *sold* for, not just what someone asked. The loop tightens.

And in the background, every night at four, three models argue about what this app should become — a flash-GLM researching, a free Kimi challenging, a Claude adjudicating — and whatever survives their disagreement becomes tomorrow's story, written down where the next agent picks it up. You never attend the meeting. You read the minutes.

That's the whole shape of it: **one inventory, priced by a platform you own, fed by a harness that never sleeps, corrected every night by a committee that costs pennies.**

The cards are worth what they're worth. Now you know, immediately, and everywhere.
